# Mode: competitors

Who competes for the campaign's audience, how each one positions itself, and the category's history. At most eight competitors per run.

## The search plan

Build the searches from the run's seed (`SKILL.md`, section 1): the company and its product, the category it is in, and the audience's pains and keywords.

- **"alternatives to"**: "alternatives to <product>", "<product> alternatives".
- **"vs"**: "<product> vs", "<competitor> vs <competitor>".
- **Category terms**: "<category> software", "<category> tools", "<category> for <audience>", and the terms the personas' keywords give.
- **Names the context already gives**: a competitor named in an internal page or a persona's objections is a search term, confirmed on that competitor's own site before it becomes a finding.

Choose at most eight competitors: those that turn up across several searches and that the audience could pick instead. Leave out the company itself, and respect the campaign's constraints (one that forbids naming competitors' customers, say).

## Per competitor

Read its homepage, its pricing page and its about page with WebFetch:

- **Its positioning, attributed**: "<Competitor> says it …", quoting its own page. A competitor's word about itself is never a fact, and on its own it is at most `medium`.
- **Its public pricing model**: a free tier, per seat, per usage or "contact sales", as its pricing page states it. "No public pricing" is a finding when the page says to contact sales. Never guess a price, and give a figure only when the quote shows it.
- **Its claims** about results, customers or awards, attributed. A claim another site confirms can be `high`, with sources on two hosts.

## Category history

When the category started, how it has changed, and the events that shaped it: launches, acquisitions, shifts in how buyers choose. Each event has a dated source, such as an announcement, a release note or a dated article, and a date appears only when the quote or the page gives it.

## Angles

With four researchers, for example:

1. The field: which competitors the searches surface, and how the category describes itself.
2. Up to four competitors: homepage, pricing and about pages.
3. The other competitors, the same way.
4. The category's history, with dated sources.

## The findings to aim for

- "<Competitor> says it <its positioning>." (`medium`, its own page)
- "<Competitor> publishes per-seat pricing, with a free tier for one project." (`medium`, its pricing page)
- "<Category> tools <a trait they share>." (`high`, two hosts)
- "<Event> in <year> <what it changed>." (a dated source)
